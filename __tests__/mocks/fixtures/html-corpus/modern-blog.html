Why We Rewrote Our Sync Engine Twice

By Priya Raman · September 14, 2025 · 9 min read

When we started building Driftwood, offline support was a checkbox on a roadmap. Three years and two rewrites later, it is the single most important piece of infrastructure we own. This is the story of how we got it wrong, got it wrong differently, and then finally settled on something boring that works.

Version one: last write wins

Our first engine stored a timestamp on every record and let the most recent write win. It was simple, it was fast, and it silently destroyed data whenever two people edited the same note on a plane. Clock skew made it worse: a laptop with a wrong clock could overwrite a week of changes with a single keystroke.

Version two: CRDTs everywhere

For the rewrite we went all in on conflict-free replicated data types. Every field became a CRDT, every list became a sequence CRDT, and merges became mathematically guaranteed to converge. They did converge. They also converged on states nobody had intended, and the metadata overhead tripled the size of our database.

Convergence is a property of the algorithm. Intent is a property of the user.

Version three: an operation log and a server

The engine we run today is an append-only operation log with a central server that assigns a total order. Clients apply operations optimistically and rebase on the server's order when they reconnect. It is less elegant than CRDTs and less clever than anything in a paper, but it is easy to reason about and easy to debug. You can read more in our design notes or browse the sync documentation.

  • Operations are idempotent and carry a client-generated id.
  • The server is the only source of ordering.
  • Rebase conflicts are surfaced to the user, never resolved silently.

If there is one lesson here, it is that the right amount of cleverness for infrastructure is usually less than you think.
