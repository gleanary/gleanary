On the Economics of Small Presses

By Harold Pinkney — March 4, 2003

Every few years somebody announces the death of the small literary press, and every few years the small presses go on publishing anyway. They print runs of five hundred copies, they pay their authors in contributor copies and gratitude, and they keep the lights on with a mixture of grants, subscriptions, and the stubbornness of a few volunteers who would rather be doing this than anything else.

The economics, on paper, make no sense. A book that sells three hundred copies at twelve dollars grosses less than four thousand dollars, and that is before the printer, the distributor, the postage, and the inevitable returns from bookstores that ordered optimistically. Nobody involved is getting rich. Most are losing money in a slow, dignified way.

And yet the presses persist. I think there are three reasons, and none of them show up on a balance sheet.

First, the cost of entry has collapsed. Desktop publishing and short-run digital printing mean that a press can exist in a spare bedroom. Second, the audience is small but intensely loyal, and loyalty is cheaper to serve than reach. Third, and most importantly, the people who run these presses measure success in a currency the market does not price: the book that would not otherwise exist.

That last point is the one economists miss. A small press is not a business that happens to publish books; it is a publishing project that happens to need a business attached to it. Read it that way and the numbers stop looking like failure and start looking like a reasonable price for something people care about.
